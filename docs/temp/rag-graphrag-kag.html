RAG, GraphRAG and KAG: which retrieval method fits which work

Essay · 24 November 2026 · Animesh Kumar

RAG, GraphRAG and KAG are three ways to give a language model the facts it needs before it answers. None of them is better in general. Each one fits some kinds of question and fails on others, at a very different cost. This page explains how each one works, what independent tests found, and how to match a method to what your design needs.

In short
  • The shape of the question chooses the method: a lookup, a chain of links, a summary of everything, or a rule (section 1).
  • Plain RAG is fast and cheap, and it is the right default for lookups. It misses links between documents and the view of the whole corpus (section 2).
  • GraphRAG and KAG build structure before the question. GraphRAG fits summaries and multi-hop questions. KAG fits rule-bound work in a stable domain. Both cost much more (sections 3 and 4).
  • In independent tests, no method wins every question type, and LLM judges inflate many published wins (section 6).
  • Start with plain RAG and a set of test questions. Add structure only for the question types that fail, and budget for the entity work that structure needs (section 9).
A note on bias: I work on DataOS at The Modern Data Company, which sells a semantic and knowledge layer. Section 8 touches that market. Its strongest evidence comes from vendors in the same market, and the page marks each one (vendor).
Who this is for: engineers, data leaders and product managers who must choose a retrieval design or judge a proposal for one. Product and data leaders can read section 1, the fit cards at the end of sections 2 to 4, and section 9. Engineers will find most use in sections 3 to 7. The page is not a tutorial and does not compare vendor products.

Terms used below

LLMA large language model, such as GPT-4o or Llama.
ChunkA short piece of a document, often 100 to 1,200 tokens.
TokenA unit of text that a model reads, about three quarters of a word.
EmbeddingA list of numbers that places a text by its meaning.
Vector searchA search for the chunks whose embeddings are nearest to the question.
Top-kThe k best matches that a search returns.
RerankerA second model that sorts the matches again, more carefully.
EntityA named thing: a supplier, a person, a contract, a country.
RelationA typed link between two entities, such as "owned by".
Knowledge graphA set of entities joined by relations.
CommunityA group of entities that link to each other more than to the rest.
SchemaThe allowed types of entity and relation, written in advance.
Logical formA question rewritten as a short program of steps.
Multi-hop questionA question that needs two or more linked facts.
Global questionA question about a whole collection, such as its main themes.
LLM judgeAn LLM that compares two answers and picks the better one.
F1 and EMAnswer scores from 0 to 100. EM counts exact matches. F1 gives part credit for overlapping words.

1Four kinds of question, four design needs

I use one invented example on the whole page. A company keeps its supplier documents in one place: contracts, audit reports, emails and complaint tickets. Four people ask four questions about the same documents. The questions look alike, but each one needs something different from the system.

The table lists the four questions, their shape, and what each one needs from the design. I invented the example.

QuestionShapeWhat the design needs
A. What payment term is in our contract with Acme Logistics?Lookup: one fact in one placeSpeed, low cost, the exact passage
B. Which suppliers share a parent company with a supplier that failed an audit?Multi-hop: facts in different documents, joined by a linkLinks between entities across documents
C. What are the main themes in supplier complaints this year?Global: a view of the whole collectionA summary of everything, not the top few chunks
D. How many German suppliers break our 60-day payment policy?Rule and count: a definition, a filter, a numberExact types, a written rule, and arithmetic

Most real systems receive all four shapes, in different shares. The share decides the design.

The rest of the page answers these four questions with each method. Plain RAG answers question A well. GraphRAG helps with C and B. KAG targets D. Long-context models and agents offer other routes, at other costs.

The point is not which method wins. The point is which method fits the share of A, B, C and D that your users ask.

Three other design needs matter as much as the question shape. The first is cost per question and the time to build the index. The second is how often the documents change. The third is the cost of a wrong answer. A support bot and a compliance tool can receive the same questions and still need different designs.

2Plain RAG: fast and cheap, blind to links and to the whole corpus

Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, joins a search step to a language model. The original paper paired a text generator with a dense index of Wikipedia, and it set new records on three open-domain question tasks (Lewis and colleagues, May 2020). Today, "RAG" usually means a simpler pipeline built around an off-the-shelf LLM.

2.1How it works

The work happens in two phases. Before any question, the system splits documents into chunks, turns each chunk into an embedding, and stores it in a vector index. At question time, it turns the question into an embedding, finds the nearest chunks, and puts them into the prompt with the question (Gao and colleagues, Dec 2023). The figure below shows both phases.

Two settings shape the results. Chunk size is a trade: chunks of 100 to 512 tokens are common, larger chunks add noise and cost, and smaller chunks lose context (Gao and colleagues, Dec 2023). A reranker and a keyword index next to the vector index recover many misses at low cost. The survey calls these additions advanced RAG.

2.2Where it fails

The first failure is placement. Models use facts at the start and end of a long prompt better than facts in the middle. With 20 documents, GPT-3.5-Turbo scored 75.8% when the answer came first and 53.8% when it sat in the middle (Liu and colleagues, Jul 2023). The middle score was lower than the 56.1% that the model scored with no documents at all.

The second failure is the multi-hop question. One news benchmark has questions that need two to four pieces of evidence. On it, GPT-4 reached 0.56 accuracy with retrieved chunks and 0.89 with the true evidence (Tang and Yang, Jan 2024). The model could reason. The search did not bring it the facts.

The third failure is the global question. Top-k search returns a few chunks, so it cannot see a theme that runs through thousands. Microsoft Research reported that baseline RAG struggles to connect the dots across a large collection (Microsoft Research, Feb 2024). An independent study listed the same weak spots: multi-step reasoning, general queries, long queries and implicit queries (Li and colleagues, Jul 2024).

2.3The four questions

Plain RAG answers question A well, because one contract chunk holds the payment term. It usually misses question B, because the audit report and the ownership record do not share words with the question. For question C, it summarizes the handful of complaints that match the wording, not the whole year. For question D, it finds some German contracts but cannot apply the rule to all of them or count them.

Good forLookups, documents that change often, a fast start, a low budget
Poor forMulti-hop links, whole-corpus summaries, rules and counts
CostThe lowest: no LLM calls to build the index, and a few hundred to a few thousand prompt tokens per question
Typical failureThe right chunk is not retrieved, or it is lost in the middle of a long prompt

3GraphRAG: build a map of the corpus first

GraphRAG is a family of methods that build a knowledge graph from the documents and search the graph as well as the text. The best-known member is Microsoft's GraphRAG, an open-source project described in a 2024 paper (Edge and colleagues, Apr 2024). This section uses it as the example. Section 5 covers lighter members of the family.

3.1How GraphRAG builds the index

The indexing pipeline has four steps, and two of them call an LLM. First, the system splits the text into chunks. In the paper they had 600 tokens with a 100-token overlap. Second, an LLM reads every chunk and extracts entities, relations and claims (GraphRAG docs, Nov 2026). Third, an algorithm called hierarchical Leiden groups the graph into nested communities. Fourth, an LLM writes a summary of each community, from the smallest groups up to the root (Edge and colleagues, Apr 2024).

The paper matched entities by their exact name, and it turned repeated mentions into heavier links (Edge and colleagues, Apr 2024). This detail matters in production. "Acme Logistics", "Acme" and "ACME Logistics GmbH" can become three entities. Section 7 returns to this.

3.2Three ways to search the graph

Local search starts from the entities in the question and reads their neighbors, their relations and the source text. Global search uses the community summaries in a map-reduce pattern. Each summary gives a partial answer with an importance score, and a final step combines the best points (GraphRAG docs, Nov 2026). The docs note that lower community levels give more thorough answers but cost more time and LLM calls.

DRIFT search mixes the two. It matches the question against community reports, drafts an answer, writes follow-up questions, and runs those through local search. On 50 local questions over more than 5,000 news articles, an LLM judge preferred DRIFT to local search on comprehensiveness 78% of the time (Microsoft Research, Oct 2024).

3.3What it improves

The GraphRAG paper tested 125 sensemaking questions on each of two corpora, of about 1 and 1.7 million tokens. The graph-based answers beat vector RAG on comprehensiveness 72-79% of the time and on diversity 62-81% of the time (Edge and colleagues, Apr 2024). The root-level summaries also answered with 9x-43x fewer tokens than a map-reduce over all the source text.

Read those numbers with care. An LLM generated the questions and an LLM judged the answers. An independent study found that a GPT-4o judge tended to favor the answer it saw first. In one order it preferred GraphRAG local search 97% of the time, and in the other order it preferred RAG 88% of the time (Han and colleagues, Feb 2025). Section 6 shows how much this bias changes published results.

3.4What it costs

Indexing is the large cost. On one multi-hop corpus of 11,656 passages, GraphRAG used 115.5 million input tokens, 36.1 million output tokens and 277 minutes. A dense embedding model indexed the same corpus in 12.1 minutes with no LLM tokens (Gutiérrez and colleagues, Feb 2025). Microsoft estimated graph building with GPT-4o-mini at about $0.34 per 30,000 words, and it advised users to start with a small sample (Microsoft Tech Community, Aug 2024).

Global search also costs a lot per question. One benchmark reported an average of about 331,375 prompt tokens for global search on its novel corpus, against about 879 for plain RAG. The same paper also gives lower figures for global search, up to about 40,000 tokens (Xiang and colleagues, Jun 2025). Another measured 521 seconds and about 354,000 tokens per global query, against 9.1 seconds and 1,680 tokens for plain RAG (Zhou and colleagues, Mar 2025). Local search is much cheaper than global search.

3.5The four questions

Global search fits question C. It reads summaries of every complaint cluster, including the ones whose words do not match the question. Local search helps question B, if the extraction linked each supplier to its parent company. For question A, GraphRAG adds cost and little else. For question D, it has no notion of a payment rule.

GraphRAG also carries a risk: on questions with no answer in the corpus, global search scored 19.27, against 96.01 for plain RAG (Han and colleagues, Feb 2025). It tends to answer anyway.

Good forGlobal and thematic questions, multi-hop questions, exploring an unfamiliar corpus
Poor forSingle facts, questions with no answer, documents that change daily, rules and counts
CostThe highest build cost in this group, and high query cost for global search
Typical failureEntities missing from the extracted graph, duplicate entities, confident answers to questions with no answer

4KAG: add a schema and logic

KAG, short for knowledge-augmented generation, is Ant Group's framework on its OpenSPG engine (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). The general idea has other names and other builds. I use Ant Group's KAG as the example because it is open source and documented. Where GraphRAG lets an LLM decide what the entities are, KAG starts from a schema and plans each answer as a set of logical steps.

4.1A mutual index between the graph and the text

KAG links each graph node back to the text chunks that support it, and stores the graph and the vectors side by side (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). A search can start in the graph and read the text, or start in the text and follow the graph. This keeps the original wording in reach. That matters because graph-only methods that drop the text chunks often do poorly (Zhou and colleagues, Mar 2025).

4.2A schema and concept alignment

KAG checks extracted facts against a schema of types and a graph of concepts. It uses relations such as synonym, isA, isPartOf, contains, belongTo and causes (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). In the example, "Acme Logistics GmbH" becomes one Supplier entity, linked to the concept Logistics and to the country Germany. The schema can also hold rules, such as the 60-day payment policy.

4.3A solver that plans the answer

The KAG solver turns a question into a logical form: a plan made of operators named Retrieval, Sort, Math, Deduce and Output (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). A later model, KAG-Thinker, is a 7B model fine-tuned on about 71,000 samples to plan the steps and decide when to retrieve (Ant Group, Jun 2025). The figure shows a plan for question D.

Plain RAG and GraphRAG leave the arithmetic and the rule to the LLM, inside one prompt. KAG makes them separate steps with their own inputs. A reviewer can read the plan and see which contracts it counted. That is the main reason to pay for this design.

4.4What the results show, and who measured them

The KAG paper compared KAG with a baseline that combined IRCoT and HippoRAG. F1 rose from 63.7 to 76.2 on HotpotQA and from 57.1 to 76.2 on 2Wiki (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). On MuSiQue, KAG reported EM 36.7 and F1 48.7 against 26.2 and 36.5. The abstract reports these gains as 19.6% and 33.5%, but the table arithmetic assigns the two figures to the opposite datasets. I cite the table.

The paper also reports professional use. In an e-government question test with 492 samples, KAG reached 91.6 precision and 71.8 recall, against 66.5 and 52.6 for naive RAG (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). These are strong numbers for question D-style work, where the answer follows rules.

All of these numbers come from Ant Group. I found no independent benchmark that includes KAG as a compared system. A benchmark by Xiao and colleagues, for example, tested nine graph methods and left KAG out (Xiao and colleagues, Jun 2025). On KAG's own 2025 leaderboard, version 0.7 almost tied PIKE-RAG on MuSiQue exact match, 0.385 against 0.383 (OpenSPG, Apr 2025).

4.5What it costs

The KAG paper says that schema-constrained knowledge construction is accurate but has a high cost in manual work (Liang and colleagues, Sep 2024). An independent test of OpenSPG, the engine under KAG, found that aligning database columns to the schema was not trivial. Mismatches caused runtime errors or silent omissions, and loading 40 KB of data took about 15 minutes on an 8 GB machine (Yaman and colleagues, Nov 2025). The authors still found value in it, but called the tooling immature.

The build cost is falling. KAG version 0.7 added a lightweight build mode with a small accuracy drop. It cut construction cost per 100,000 characters from 4.63 to 0.479 yuan, about 90% lower (OpenSPG, Apr 2025). That number is self-reported.

4.6The four questions

KAG fits question D, if the schema has suppliers, contracts, countries and the payment rule. It helps question B, because the schema makes "parent company" a typed relation instead of a guess. It is too heavy for question A. For question C, it offers no clear advantage over GraphRAG's summaries.

Good forRule-bound professional work in a stable domain: compliance, policy, finance, medicine
Poor forOpen or fast-changing corpora, a quick start, teams with no one to own the schema
CostSchema design and upkeep, many LLM calls to build and to solve, immature tooling
Typical failureThe schema does not match the data, and facts drop out silently

5The wider family

Many methods sit between plain RAG and KAG, and some sit outside the line. The map below places eleven of them on two axes. The first axis is how much structure the system builds before the question. The second is the cost of each question.

Neither axis is a score. A method in the bottom left is not better than one in the top right. It fits different work.

Select a dot to see a short summary.

Two patterns stand out. First, the structure axis and the cost axis are separate. HippoRAG 2 builds a graph and still answers cheaply, while long context builds nothing and costs the most per question. Second, the right-hand column is thin. Only KAG builds structure from a schema that people write, and its evidence is all self-reported.

5.1LazyGraphRAG

Buildsa concept graph with no LLMQuery costtunableFromMicrosoft Research, 2024

LazyGraphRAG replaces the LLM extraction step with plain language-processing tools. It finds noun phrases and their co-occurrences, and it builds communities from graph statistics. All LLM work moves to question time (Microsoft Research, Nov 2024).

Microsoft says that LazyGraphRAG indexes at 0.1% of the cost of full GraphRAG, the same as vector RAG. It also reports quality comparable to GraphRAG global search on global questions at more than 700x lower query cost (Microsoft Research, Nov 2024). The test used one news dataset, 100 synthetic questions and an LLM judge. I found no independent test.

People confuse it with a cheaper version of the same graph. It is a different graph: concepts and co-occurrences, not typed entities and relations. It cannot answer question B by following a "parent company" link, because no such link exists.

Fits exploration on a budgetIt fits global questions over a large corpus when the build budget is small. The quality claims need an independent test.

AskDo our questions need typed relations, or only themes and co-occurrence?

5.2LightRAG

Buildsan entity graph with an LLMQuery costdisputedFromHKU, 2024

LightRAG searches with two levels of keywords. Low-level keywords match entities, and high-level keywords match relations. It adds new documents to the graph without a rebuild (Guo and colleagues, Oct 2024). Incremental updates are a real need, so this design choice matters.

The LightRAG paper claims retrieval with fewer than 100 tokens and one API call. An independent benchmark reported an average of about 100,832 prompt tokens, although the same paper also gives a figure near 10,000 (Xiang and colleagues, Jun 2025). The paper's own overall win rates against GraphRAG were only about 50-55%, judged by GPT-4o-mini. In an independent rerun on question-answer benchmarks, LightRAG scored 6.6 average F1, against 57.0 for a dense retriever (Gutiérrez and colleagues, Feb 2025). Those authors compete with LightRAG, so their result needs a third test, but the gap is very large.

When a study removed position and length bias from the LLM judge, LightRAG's win rate over naive RAG fell from 66.70% to 39.06% (Zeng and colleagues, May 2025).

Claims ahead of evidenceThe incremental update is useful. The cost and quality claims did not survive independent tests.

AskDid anyone outside the authors measure the tokens per question?

5.3HippoRAG 2

Buildsan entity graph with an LLMQuery costlowFromOhio State, 2024 and 2025

HippoRAG takes its design from a theory of how the brain indexes memories. It builds a knowledge graph with an LLM, then ranks passages with Personalized PageRank, a graph walk that starts from the entities in the question (Gutiérrez and colleagues, May 2024). The first version claimed up to 20% gains on multi-hop questions, and it matched an iterative method while it cost 10-30x less.

HippoRAG 2 averaged 59.8 F1 across seven benchmarks, against 57.0 for a strong dense retriever and 49.6 for GraphRAG (Gutiérrez and colleagues, Feb 2025). Its index used 9.2 million input tokens on the corpus where GraphRAG used 115.5 million, and it answered in 1.2 seconds. The authors also say that earlier graph methods fall well below standard RAG on simple factual questions.

The gain over a good dense retriever is small: 2.8 F1 points. A team that already has strong embeddings may not see a large change.

Fits multi-hop at low costIt is the cheapest graph method here at question time, and it does not give up simple lookups. The results come from its own authors.

AskHow much better is it than our current dense retriever on our own questions?

5.4Iterative and agentic retrieval

Buildsnothing extraQuery costseveral LLM callsFromIRCoT, 2022, and many agent designs

Iterative retrieval lets the model search, reason one step, and search again with what it learned. IRCoT alternates retrieval with chain-of-thought steps. With GPT-3, it improved retrieval by up to 21 points and answers by up to 15 points on four multi-hop benchmarks (Trivedi and colleagues, Dec 2022). Agentic RAG extends the idea: an agent chooses tools, writes queries and decides when it has enough.

For question B, an agent can search for audit failures, read the supplier names, then search for each parent company. It needs no graph, but it needs several LLM calls per question. I found no independent cost or latency numbers for agentic RAG.

People often confuse it with GraphRAG, because both handle multi-hop questions. The difference is when the system finds the links. GraphRAG finds them once, at index time. An agent finds them again for every question.

Fits rare multi-hop questionsIt fits when multi-hop questions are rare or varied, so a graph is not worth building. Cost and delay grow with every step.

AskHow many searches does a typical question take, and who caps them?

5.5Long context and Self-Route

BuildsnothingQuery costthe highest per questionFrommodels with very large windows

A long-context model reads the whole document set in one prompt, so it needs no retrieval. With enough budget, long context beat RAG on average: GPT-4o scored 48.67 against 32.60 (Li and colleagues, Jul 2024). The same study proposed Self-Route. The model first tries RAG and falls back to long context only when the chunks are not enough. Self-Route matched long-context accuracy with GPT-4o and used about 61% of the tokens.

Other results point the other way. Order-preserving RAG with 48K tokens beat a full 117K-token long context on one benchmark, 47.25 F1 against 34.26 (Yu and colleagues, Sep 2024). The vendor Databricks ran over 2,000 experiments. Most models did worse beyond a certain length, for example after 32K tokens for Llama-3.1-405b (Databricks, Aug 2024). On SQL-like tasks, Gemini 1.5 Pro with long context scored 0.38, against 0.65 for a dedicated pipeline (Lee and colleagues, Jun 2024).

Long context fits a small, stable corpus and a modest volume of questions. It does not remove the need for rules and counts, which is question D.

Fits small corporaIt is the simplest design when everything fits in the window. The numbers age fast with each model release.

AskDoes the whole corpus fit, and what does each question cost at that size?

5.6Cache-augmented generation

Buildsa cached promptQuery costlow after the first loadFromChan and colleagues, 2024

Cache-augmented generation, or CAG, loads all documents into the context once and keeps the model's internal state in a cache. Each question reuses that state, so there is no retrieval step. On HotPotQA, CAG beat the best RAG setup on the small and medium sets, but lost on the large set of 85,000 tokens (Chan and colleagues, Dec 2024).

The limit is plain: all documents must fit in the context. The authors say that this is impractical for much larger datasets.

Fits small, fixed knowledgeIt fits a product manual or a policy set that changes rarely. It does not fit a growing document store.

AskHow often does the cached knowledge change?

5.7Routing and combining

Buildstwo or more indexesQuery costdepends on the routeFromseveral 2024 and 2025 studies

If no method wins everywhere, a system can send each question to the method that fits it. On one multi-hop benchmark, 13.6% of questions were answered only by GraphRAG and 11.6% only by RAG (Han and colleagues, Feb 2025). Combining the two outputs improved on the best single method by 6.4%. Self-Route, in 5.5, is a simpler form of the same idea.

The cost is more moving parts: two indexes to keep current, and a router that can send a question the wrong way.

Fits mixed question loadsIt follows directly from the benchmark evidence in section 6. I think it is the design to test first when users ask more than one shape of question.

AskHow does the router decide, and how do we know when it routed wrongly?

6What independent benchmarks say

Three independent studies from 2025 compared plain RAG and graph methods under one protocol. They agree on the main pattern. Graph methods help on multi-hop, comparison, time-based and summary questions. Plain RAG ties or wins on single facts. The matrix below collects the results by question type.

Plain RAG wins on single facts

Under a unified protocol, plain RAG scored 64.78 F1 on single-hop questions from Natural Questions, against 63.01 for GraphRAG local search (Han and colleagues, Feb 2025). On one multi-hop benchmark, GraphRAG local search won time-based questions, 50.60 against 30.70. Plain RAG won inference questions and questions with no answer. A benchmark by Xiang and colleagues found the same split. On a corpus of novels, GraphRAG beat RAG on complex reasoning, 50.93 against 42.93, but not on simple fact retrieval. On a medical corpus, RAG with reranking won complex reasoning, 58.64 against 47.04 (Xiang and colleagues, Jun 2025).

Graph methods help, but often by little

A third benchmark used 1,018 college-level questions. There, the graph methods beat simple keyword search (BM25) by less than two accuracy points, and three of nine fell below it (Xiao and colleagues, Jun 2025). A study of 12 graph methods in one framework found that methods that drop the text chunks and keep only the graph often perform poorly (Zhou and colleagues, Mar 2025). Structure helps most when it sits next to the text, not in place of it.

LLM judges inflate many published wins

Many graph papers report win rates from an LLM judge. Judges prefer the answer they see first and the longer answer. One study found that position changed a method's win rate against itself by more than 30 points (Zeng and colleagues, May 2025). After the authors removed the biases, the gains of graph methods became much more moderate or vanished. When a paper reports only LLM-judged win rates, treat its gains as an upper bound.

Test on your own questions

The benchmarks disagree in detail because their corpora and questions differ. The useful habit is to build a set of your own test questions, labeled by shape (A, B, C, D, and no-answer), with checked answers. Score exact answers where you can. Where you need an LLM judge, swap the answer order and average both runs.

7How graph-based systems fail in production

The benchmarks measure clean datasets. Production adds messy names, daily updates and access rules. These are the failures that the sources document.

The extracted graph misses entities

In one independent study, only about 65% of answer entities appeared in the knowledge graphs that an LLM extracted (Han and colleagues, Feb 2025). A search over the graph alone cannot find the rest. On single-hop questions, a triplet-only graph method scored 34.28 F1, against 64.78 for plain RAG. Better extraction models raised accuracy on one benchmark from 71.17 to 75.08, so graph quality is a lever.

The same thing appears under many names

LLM-built graphs hold duplicates such as "LLM", "LLMs" and "Large Language Models". Entity resolution, the work of matching these names to one entity, removed about 40% of entities in one study and still improved answers (Zheng and colleagues, Oct 2025). In the supplier example, this is the difference between one Acme Logistics and three.

Updates create duplicates and stale facts

A batch pipeline matches names only inside each new batch. A practitioner describes how a new spelling of a company name becomes a separate node, cut off from the original node with its 847 links (DEV Community, 2025). Old facts also stay next to new ones, such as two head-office addresses, unless each fact carries the dates when it was true. The post describes the problem and its remedies, not a measured study.

Summaries can leak restricted content

Community summaries combine entities from many documents. One course page says that Microsoft GraphRAG 2.x has no built-in role-based access control for communities. So a summary can carry facts from a restricted document to a user without access (The Neural Base, Apr 2026). This source has no named author and no measurements, but the mechanism follows from how summaries are built. Plain RAG can filter chunks by permission before the prompt. A summary has no single owner to filter on.

Confident answers to questions with no answer

Global search answered questions that had no answer in the corpus. It scored 19.27 on those questions, against 96.01 for plain RAG (Han and colleagues, Feb 2025). For a compliance tool, a confident wrong answer costs more than "I do not know".

Build time at company scale

SAP estimated the cost to build a graph with GPT-4o for one company corpus. It came to about 800,000 API calls and 65.7 days in series (Min and colleagues, Aug 2025). A graph built with a classic dependency parser, with no LLM, reached 94% of the quality of the GPT-4o graph. The cheap graph is often good enough.

8What the structured methods depend on

GraphRAG and KAG share one dependency: they are only as good as their entities, relations and rules. Section 7 shows what goes wrong when an LLM extracts them and nobody checks the result. This section gives my view on that dependency, and the evidence on both sides.

In my view, curated identities, definitions and rules are a design input that the structured methods depend on. They are worth the cost only where the work needs exact identity, joins or rules. By curated, I mean that people own them: one ID per supplier, one definition of "payment term", one written policy. This view is narrower than the claim I started with. I first thought that the knowledge layer is the lasting asset whichever method sits on top. The research did not support that broad form.

Evidence for the view

Automatic extraction loses about a third of answer entities, and entity resolution and better extraction both raise accuracy (sections 7 and 6). KAG's results, where they exist, come from a schema-first design. Vendors that sell semantic layers report large gains on questions over tables. The vendor data.world reported that GPT-4 answered insurance questions with 16% accuracy on raw SQL and 54% over a knowledge graph (data.world, Nov 2023). With ontology-based query checks and an LLM step that rewrites bad queries, accuracy rose to 72% (data.world, May 2024). The vendor Cube reported that a 4 KB hand-written semantic document raised text-to-SQL accuracy by 17 to 23 points across three models (Cube, Apr 2026).

These vendor results are about questions over database tables, not over text. I include them because question D in a real company often spans both. All three vendors sell in the same market as my employer.

Evidence against the view

Curation costs people's time, and KAG's own paper says that the manual work costs a lot (4.5).

Newer models close part of the gap. The vendor dbt Labs reran the same insurance benchmark. Plain text-to-SQL rose from 32.7% to 64.5% by 2026 (dbt Labs, Apr 2026). On questions that the semantic layer covered, it scored 100%, against 51-63% for text-to-SQL. But on questions that needed joins outside the layer, the layer scored 0% and one text-to-SQL model scored 100%. A curated layer is exact inside its scope and blind outside it.

Cheap structure is often enough. LazyGraphRAG and SAP's parser-built graph avoid most LLM costs (5.1 and section 7). Graph methods beat keyword search by less than two points on one large benchmark (section 6). If the questions are mostly lookups, curation buys little.

What follows for the design

The view leads to a modest rule. Curate the few entity types and rules that your multi-hop and rule-bound questions touch, and leave the rest to extraction or to plain RAG. In the supplier example, that means one owned list of suppliers with their parent companies and countries, and the payment policy written as a rule. It does not mean an ontology of the whole company.

A staged path

I think four stages work best. Each one starts only when the previous one shows a gap.

  1. Build plain RAG with a keyword index and a reranker. Write a test set of real questions, labeled by shape, with checked answers.
  2. Score the test set by shape. If lookups pass and other shapes fail, find out which shape fails and how often users ask it.
  3. Add one structured method for the failing shape only. Route the other questions to plain RAG.
  4. Curate the entity types and rules that the structured method touches, and assign an owner to each one.

The order matters because the cost rises at each stage. A team that starts at stage 4 pays for curation too early. It does not yet know which questions need it.

A team that never leaves stage 1 keeps receiving wrong answers to questions B, C and D. It may not notice, because the answers sound right.
