On Monday morning, Lantern's finance director sends one question to the data team: "How much dog food did we sell in each region last quarter, and how does that compare with grooming revenue in the same stores?" The data to answer it exists. Sales are in the store POS and the web shop. Grooming revenue is in the booking system. Regions are in a spreadsheet that the operations team maintains.

Without a model, an analyst joins a dozen source tables, guesses which store code means what, and hopes that the two systems agree on what a quarter is. With a star schema, the same question is one short query that any analyst can read. The star schema is the most widely used analytical model for exactly this reason. It shapes data around the questions that people ask, not around the systems that produced it.

This chapter builds Lantern's sales star from nothing. It starts with the design process, then covers the two table types, facts and dimensions, in detail. Then it loads the star, queries it, and connects it to other stars. Chapter 8 continues with the advanced techniques, such as slowly changing dimensions and bridge tables.

NoteThis chapter uses the Lantern Pet Supply case from chapter 2. You need only a short version here. Lantern is a US pet retailer with 85 stores, a web shop, and grooming salons in 40 stores. It sells about 25,000 products and takes about 40,000 orders a day. Customer data comes from a CRM, a web shop, and a store POS.

7.1The four-step design process

A star schema does not start from a source table. It starts from a business process: an activity that the company performs and measures. Ralph Kimball's method designs each star in four steps, always in the same order. Each step depends on the one before it.

  1. Choose the business process. Pick one activity that produces measurements, such as taking an order, receiving stock, or completing a grooming appointment.
  2. Declare the grain. Say in one sentence what one row of the fact table means.
  3. Identify the dimensions. List the who, what, where, when, and how that describe each row at that grain.
  4. Identify the facts. List the numeric measurements that belong to each row at that grain.

Table 7.1 The four steps, applied to Lantern's sales

StepDecision for Lantern sales
1 Business processSelling products, in stores and on the web shop
2 GrainOne row per order line: one product on one order
3 DimensionsDate, customer, product, store, promotion, payment type
4 FactsQuantity, gross amount, discount amount, net amount, cost amount

The order matters. Teams that skip to step 4 and list every number they can find end up with a table whose rows mean different things. The grain in step 2 is the contract. Every dimension and every fact must be true for exactly one order line. A store's monthly rent, for example, fails that test. It is a real number, but it does not belong to one order line, so it belongs in a different star.

TipChoose the most atomic grain that the source can give you. Lantern's POS records every order line, so the star stores every order line. You can always add up fine rows into coarse totals. You can never split a coarse total back into the rows it came from.

Where the star came from

Ralph Kimball described dimensional modeling in The Data Warehouse Toolkit, first published in 1996. The third edition, written with Margy Ross, appeared in 2013. The Kimball Group also keeps a free list of dimensional modeling techniques, with short definitions of every term in this chapter. The method was a reaction to large, normalized enterprise models that took years to deliver. Kimball's answer was to deliver one business process at a time, in a shape that business users could read.

7.2Facts

A fact is a measurement of a business event. A fact table holds one row per event at the declared grain. Each row has a foreign key to every dimension, plus the measurements themselves. Fact tables are narrow and very long. Lantern's sales fact gains about 100,000 rows a day.

7.2.1Which measures you can add up

The most useful property of a fact is whether you can add it up. The answer decides which queries give correct totals. Measures fall into three groups.

Table 7.2 Additive, semi-additive, and non-additive measures at Lantern

KindLantern exampleCan you add it up?
AdditiveNet amount of an order lineYes, across every dimension: products, stores, customers, and days
Semi-additiveQuantity on hand in a store at closing timeAcross stores and products, yes. Across days, no. Ten units on Monday and ten on Tuesday is not twenty units.
Non-additiveUnit price, margin percentageNo. Adding two prices or two percentages gives a meaningless number.

For a non-additive measure, store its additive parts instead. Lantern does not store a margin percentage on each sales row. It stores net_amount and cost_amount, which both add up. A query then computes the margin from the totals.

WarningNever average a stored percentage. If store A has a 50% margin on 10 USD of sales and store B has a 10% margin on 1,000 USD, the average of the two percentages is 30%. The true margin of the two stores together is about 10.4%. Add up the parts first, then divide.

7.2.2Four kinds of fact table

Business processes do not all behave the same way. Some are single events, some are states measured at intervals, and some are pipelines with several steps. Dimensional modeling has a fact table type for each.

A transaction fact table records one event per row. fact_sales_line is one. A row appears when the event happens and does not change afterwards. Most fact tables are of this type.

A periodic snapshot records a state at a regular interval. fact_inventory_daily holds one row per product, per store, per day, with the quantity on hand at closing time. A row exists even on a day with no sales, so you can see that a product sat on a shelf for a month. Its measures are often semi-additive.

An accumulating snapshot follows a process through its milestones. fact_grooming_appointment holds one row per appointment. The row has one date key per milestone: booked, checked in, groomed, and paid. The load updates the same row as each milestone happens. This is the one fact table type that updates rows, and it answers questions about lags, such as "how many days pass between booking and visit?"

A factless fact table records that something happened or was true, with no measure. fact_promotion_coverage holds one row per product, per store, per day that the product was on promotion. Joined with sales, it answers a question that the sales fact alone cannot: which products were on promotion but did not sell?

7.3Dimensions

A dimension holds the context of the facts: everything a user filters, groups, or labels by. Where facts are narrow and long, dimensions are wide and short. Lantern's product dimension has about 25,000 rows and more than 40 columns.

7.3.1Wide, flat, and in plain words

A good dimension describes its subject in the words that users say. The source stores a category code such as DOG_DRY. The dimension stores "Dry dog food". It also stores the department, the brand, the pack size, the life stage ("Puppy", "Adult", "Senior"), and a flag for prescription diets. Each of these becomes a filter or a column header in a report, with no lookup table and no code book.

A dimension also flattens hierarchies. A product belongs to a category, a category to a department. A store belongs to a district, a district to a region. In a star, every level of each hierarchy is a plain column on the dimension row. Repeating "Dog" on 6,000 product rows costs a little storage. It saves every user from learning the hierarchy.

7.3.2Surrogate keys

Every dimension row has a surrogate key: an integer that the warehouse assigns and that means nothing outside it. Lantern's customer dimension keeps the CRM's C-000123 as a normal column, but joins on customer_key = 48211. There are four reasons for this.

A dimension can hold several versions of one customer. When Maria moves, chapter 8 shows how the dimension adds a new row for her. Each version needs its own key, and a business key cannot do that.

Sources disagree about keys. The CRM, the web shop, and the POS each identify Maria differently. The surrogate key is one key for the matched person.

The warehouse needs rows that no source has. The "Unknown customer" row in section 7.3.4 needs a key.

Integer joins are small and fast. A fact table with 40 million rows a year carries six foreign keys on every row. Four-byte integers keep the table small.

7.3.3The date dimension

Almost every star has a date dimension, and it is the easiest one to build. It has one row per calendar day, created in advance for many years. Its key is often the date written as a number, such as 20250520. That keeps the key readable and still an integer.

Its value is in the columns. Lantern, like many US retailers, reports on a 4-5-4 fiscal calendar: each quarter has a 4-week month, a 5-week month, and a 4-week month, and the fiscal year starts in February. No analyst can compute that in a query. The date dimension stores it once.

Listing 7.1 The Lantern date dimension

CREATE TABLE dim_date (
  date_key          INTEGER     PRIMARY KEY,  -- 202505201
  calendar_date     DATE        NOT NULL,
  day_name          VARCHAR(9)  NOT NULL,     -- 'Tuesday'
  is_weekend        BOOLEAN     NOT NULL,
  is_us_holiday     BOOLEAN     NOT NULL,2
  fiscal_year       INTEGER     NOT NULL,     -- starts in February3
  fiscal_quarter    INTEGER     NOT NULL,
  fiscal_month      INTEGER     NOT NULL,     -- 4-5-4 weeks
  fiscal_week       INTEGER     NOT NULL,
  year_month        CHAR(7)     NOT NULL      -- '2025-05', for calendar reports
);
  1. 1A readable integer key. You can still join on it and sort by it.
  2. 2Flags that no query can derive on its own. Holiday sales are a common Lantern question.
  3. 3The fiscal calendar lives here, once. Every star that joins dim_date gets the same quarters.

7.3.4Unknown and not-applicable rows

Some facts arrive without a value for a dimension. A cash sale at the till has no known customer. A web order has no store cashier. Leaving the foreign key empty looks simple. But a null key drops the row from any inner join, and the sales total silently shrinks.

Instead, each dimension has a few special rows with fixed keys. Key -1 is "Unknown", for a value that exists but is missing. Key -2 is "Not applicable", for a value that cannot exist. Every fact row then has a real key for every dimension, and every report total adds up.

NoteKeep the two special rows apart. "Unknown customer" on a store sale is a data quality problem that someone fixes. "Not applicable cashier" on a web order is correct. A report that mixes them hides the problem.

7.4The Lantern sales star

Figure 7.1 puts the pieces together. The fact table sits in the middle, and each dimension is one join away.

Two columns in the fact are not keys to a dimension table. order_id is the order number, and lot_number is the production batch of the product. Each is a degenerate dimension: a dimension value with no other attributes, so it lives on the fact row itself. You still group and filter by it. "Show every line of order W-77810" and "find every sale of lot L24-0917" both use these columns.

Listing 7.2 The sales fact and the customer dimension

CREATE TABLE dim_customer (
  customer_key     INTEGER      PRIMARY KEY,   -- surrogate key1
  customer_id      VARCHAR(20)  NOT NULL,      -- 'C-000123', the business key
  customer_name    VARCHAR(100),
  city             VARCHAR(60),
  loyalty_tier     VARCHAR(20),
  customer_segment VARCHAR(30)                 -- 'Multi-pet household'
);

CREATE TABLE fact_sales_line (
  -- Grain: one row per order line.2
  date_key         INTEGER  NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_date,
  customer_key     INTEGER  NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_customer,3
  product_key      INTEGER  NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_product,
  store_key        INTEGER  NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_store,
  promotion_key    INTEGER  NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_promotion,
  payment_type_key INTEGER  NOT NULL REFERENCES dim_payment_type,
  order_id         VARCHAR(20) NOT NULL,          -- degenerate dimension4
  order_line_no    SMALLINT    NOT NULL,
  lot_number       VARCHAR(20),
  quantity         INTEGER       NOT NULL,          -- additive5
  net_amount       DECIMAL(12,2) NOT NULL,
  discount_amount  DECIMAL(12,2) NOT NULL,
  cost_amount      DECIMAL(12,2) NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (order_id, order_line_no)
);
  1. 1The surrogate key from section 7.3.2. The CRM's key stays as an ordinary column for lookups.
  2. 2Write the grain as a comment on the table. It is the one rule that every load and every query depends on.
  3. 3NOT NULL on every dimension key. Missing values point at the Unknown row instead.
  4. 4Degenerate dimensions: order number and lot number, stored on the fact.
  5. 5Only additive measures. Margin percentage is computed in queries from net_amount and cost_amount.

7.5Loading a star

A star loads in a fixed order: dimensions first, then facts. The reason is the surrogate key. A staged sales row from the POS carries the CRM customer ID, a SKU, and a store code. The fact row needs the matching surrogate keys. So the load must find each key in its dimension before it can insert the fact. Figure 7.2 shows the order.

Listing 7.3 Load the sales fact with surrogate key lookups

INSERT INTO fact_sales_line
  (date_key, customer_key, product_key, store_key, promotion_key, payment_type_key,
   order_id, order_line_no, lot_number, quantity, net_amount, discount_amount, cost_amount)
SELECT
  CAST(REPLACE(CAST(s.order_date AS VARCHAR(10)), '-', '') AS INTEGER),1
  COALESCE(c.customer_key, -1),                  -- Unknown customer2
  COALESCE(p.product_key,  -1),
  COALESCE(st.store_key,   -1),
  COALESCE(pr.promotion_key, -2),                 -- Not applicable: no promotion
  pt.payment_type_key,
  s.order_id, s.order_line_no, s.lot_number,
  s.quantity, s.net_amount, s.discount_amount, s.cost_amount
FROM stg_sales_line s
LEFT JOIN dim_customer c                             -- LEFT JOIN keeps unmatched rows3
       ON  c.customer_id = s.customer_id
      AND s.order_date BETWEEN c.valid_from AND c.valid_to  -- the version valid that day4
LEFT JOIN dim_product   p  ON p.sku            = s.sku
LEFT JOIN dim_store     st ON st.store_code     = s.store_code
LEFT JOIN dim_promotion pr ON pr.promo_code     = s.promo_code
JOIN      dim_payment_type pt ON pt.payment_code = s.payment_code;
  1. 1Turns 2025-05-20 into the date key 20250520. Many teams look this up in dim_date instead, which also checks that the date exists.
  2. 2A staged row with no matching customer gets the Unknown row, so the sale still counts in every total.
  3. 3An inner join here would silently drop sales whose customer is missing. That is the most common star loading bug.
  4. 4Chapter 8 adds version dates to the customer dimension. Joining on the order date picks the version that was valid when the sale happened, which also handles facts that arrive late.

7.5.1Facts that arrive before their dimension

Listing 7.3 sends an unmatched customer to the Unknown row. That keeps totals right, but it loses the link. Suppose a new web shop customer places an order at 23:50, and the CRM sends that customer's record the next morning. Tonight's sale points at Unknown forever, unless someone fixes it.

The common fix is an inferred member. When the fact load finds a business key with no dimension row, it first inserts a placeholder row for that key, with the attributes set to "Pending". The fact gets the placeholder's real surrogate key. When the CRM record arrives, the dimension load fills in the placeholder row in place. The fact never changes, and from the next morning on, it points at a complete customer.

Listing 7.4 Insert inferred members before the fact load

INSERT INTO dim_customer (customer_key, customer_id, customer_name, city, loyalty_tier, is_inferred)
SELECT NEXT VALUE FOR seq_customer_key,
       s.customer_id, 'Pending', 'Pending', 'Pending', TRUE1
FROM  (SELECT DISTINCT customer_id FROM stg_sales_line
       WHERE customer_id IS NOT NULL) s                  -- a known ID that the dimension lacks2
WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM dim_customer c
                  WHERE c.customer_id = s.customer_id);
  1. 1The flag tells the next dimension load to overwrite this row when the real record arrives, instead of adding a new version.
  2. 2Only rows with an ID. A cash sale with no customer ID at all still goes to the Unknown row.

7.6Querying a star

The finance director's question from the start of the chapter now takes a few lines. Every query on a star has the same shape: start from the fact, join the dimensions that you filter or group by, and add up the measures.

Listing 7.5 Dog food sales by region, last fiscal quarter

SELECT st.region,
       SUM(f.net_amount)                              AS net_sales,
       SUM(f.net_amount - f.cost_amount)              AS margin,
       SUM(f.net_amount - f.cost_amount)
         / NULLIF(SUM(f.net_amount), 0)                AS margin_pct1
FROM   fact_sales_line f
JOIN   dim_date    d  ON d.date_key    = f.date_key
JOIN   dim_product p  ON p.product_key = f.product_key
JOIN   dim_store   st ON st.store_key  = f.store_key
WHERE  p.department    = 'Dog'                           -- a plain column, no hierarchy walk2
AND    p.category_group = 'Food'
AND    d.fiscal_year    = 2025 AND d.fiscal_quarter = 2  -- the 4-5-4 quarter
GROUP BY st.region;
  1. 1The margin percentage is computed from the totals, as section 7.2.1 requires. NULLIF avoids a division by zero.
  2. 2Department and category group are flattened onto dim_product, so the filter needs no extra tables.

Semi-additive facts need one extra step. To report average stock per month, you first add up stock across stores for each day, and then average those daily totals across the days of the month.

Listing 7.6 Average daily stock of dog food, by fiscal month

SELECT fiscal_month, AVG(units_on_hand) AS avg_daily_units
FROM (
  SELECT d.fiscal_month, d.date_key,
         SUM(i.quantity_on_hand) AS units_on_hand   -- add across stores and products1
  FROM   fact_inventory_daily i
  JOIN   dim_date    d ON d.date_key    = i.date_key
  JOIN   dim_product p ON p.product_key = i.product_key
  WHERE  p.department = 'Dog' AND d.fiscal_year = 2025
  GROUP BY d.fiscal_month, d.date_key
) daily
GROUP BY fiscal_month;                                -- then average across days2
  1. 1Stock adds up across stores and products on one day.
  2. 2Across days, it is averaged, not added. Adding it counts the same units once per day.

7.6.1Two fact tables in one question

The second half of the finance question compares sales with grooming revenue per store. Grooming revenue lives in a different fact table. The tempting query joins both fact tables to dim_store at once. It gives wrong numbers.

WarningNever join two fact tables directly, or through a shared dimension, in one FROM clause. Each store has thousands of sales rows and hundreds of grooming rows. Joining them pairs every sales row with every grooming row of the same store, and both sums grow many times over. This error is called a fan trap.

The correct method is called drilling across. Total each fact separately to the same grain, here store and fiscal quarter. Then join the two small results on the shared dimension attributes.

Listing 7.7 Sales and grooming revenue per store, drilled across

WITH sales AS (
  SELECT f.store_key, SUM(f.net_amount) AS product_sales1
  FROM   fact_sales_line f JOIN dim_date d ON d.date_key = f.date_key
  WHERE  d.fiscal_year = 2025 AND d.fiscal_quarter = 2
  GROUP BY f.store_key
), grooming AS (
  SELECT g.store_key, SUM(g.amount_paid) AS grooming_revenue
  FROM   fact_grooming_appointment g JOIN dim_date d ON d.date_key = g.paid_date_key
  WHERE  d.fiscal_year = 2025 AND d.fiscal_quarter = 2
  GROUP BY g.store_key
)
SELECT st.store_name, st.region, s.product_sales, g.grooming_revenue
FROM   dim_store st
LEFT JOIN sales    s ON s.store_key = st.store_key           -- one row per store on each side2
LEFT JOIN grooming g ON g.store_key = st.store_key
WHERE  st.store_key > 0;                                -- skip the special rows
  1. 1Each fact is totaled on its own, down to one row per store.
  2. 2Now each side has one row per store, so the join cannot multiply anything. Stores without a salon show an empty grooming value.

Listing 7.7 works only because both facts use the same dim_store and the same dim_date. If the grooming star has its own store table with different codes, there is nothing to join on. That requirement has a name.

7.7Conformed dimensions, the bus matrix, and galaxies

A conformed dimension is one dimension that several fact tables share, with the same keys, the same rows, and the same meaning. Lantern's dim_store is conformed when the sales star, the grooming star, and the inventory star all use it. Then "store 14" means the same store in every report, and any two facts can be drilled across.

The same idea applies to measures. A conformed fact has one definition everywhere. If finance and marketing both report "net sales", both must subtract discounts and exclude tax in the same way, or their numbers will never match.

Kimball plans conformance with the bus matrix. Each row is a business process, and each column is a dimension. A mark means that the process uses the dimension. A column with marks in several rows is a conformed dimension, and the team must build it once and share it. The bus matrix is the plan for a whole warehouse, delivered one star at a time.

7.7.1Galaxy schemas

Once several stars share conformed dimensions, the whole model is called a galaxy schema, or a fact constellation. Figure 7.3 shows three of Lantern's fact tables and the dimensions they share.

The grooming fact does not use dim_product, which is why it has no line to it. A galaxy is not a different pattern from the star. It is what a dimensional warehouse becomes when it grows past one business process. Its strength and its risk come from the same place: one change to a shared dimension reaches every fact that uses it.

7.8History in a star

Facts rarely change. A sale on 20 May stays a sale on 20 May. When a fact is wrong, the usual fix is a correcting row: a return or a reversal with negative amounts, dated when the correction happened. The original row stays, so last month's report still matches what it showed last month.

Dimensions do change. Maria moves from Oakland to San Jose. A product changes brand. A store moves to a new district. Each change raises a question: do old sales show the old value or the new one? Dimensional modeling answers with a set of standard techniques called slowly changing dimensions. They are the main subject of chapter 8, together with bridge tables for many-to-many relationships and bitemporal dimensions for audits.

One limit is worth knowing now. A star records history as of the load. If a source corrects a value, the star usually overwrites or versions the row, and the earlier belief is gone unless the design keeps a second set of dates. Chapter 8 shows that technique, and chapter 16 shows its cost.

7.9Common mistakes

Mixing grains in one fact table. Order headers and order lines in the same table make every sum wrong, because the header's shipping fee repeats on every line. Give each grain its own fact table.

Using business keys as fact foreign keys. A fact keyed on the CRM's C-000123 cannot point at a customer version, and it breaks when a second source arrives with different IDs.

Leaving dimension keys null. Null keys drop rows from inner joins without any error. Use the Unknown and Not applicable rows from section 7.3.4.

Adding up semi-additive measures over time. Stock, balances, and headcount must be averaged or taken at period end across days, as in Listing 7.6.

Building one star per report. Stars built for single reports, each with its own customer and store tables, cannot be drilled across. The result is a set of disconnected data marts that disagree. The bus matrix exists to prevent this.

Normalizing dimensions by habit. Splitting every hierarchy into separate tables turns the star into a snowflake, with more joins for every query. Chapter 9 covers when that trade is worth it.

7.10Strengths and limits

The star is the pattern that analysts find easiest to use. Every question has the same shape: one fact, a few dimensions, a sum. The model speaks the business's language, because dimensions store "Dry dog food", not DOG_DRY. Queries are fast, because each join is one hop on a small integer key. Teams can deliver it one business process at a time, and each star is useful on the day it ships.

Its limits come from the same design. A star decides meaning while it loads. It matches customers across sources, resolves codes, and fixes the grain before any data enters. That makes the integration work heavy whenever a new source arrives, and a wrong decision is expensive to undo, because it is baked into millions of fact rows. Relationships are fixed by the fact grain, so a change from one owner per pet to two owners per pet needs a bridge table and a restated fact. Records of what the warehouse believed on a past date need extra design, and personal data is copied into every version of a dimension row.

Choose the star when analysts and dashboards are the main users, the sources are few and stable, and the questions are well known. It is also the best final layer on top of other patterns, as chapter 17 shows.

Summary

  • A star schema shapes data around business processes and the questions people ask, not around source systems.
  • Design each star in four steps: choose the business process, declare the grain, identify the dimensions, and identify the facts. The grain is the contract for everything else.
  • Facts are additive, semi-additive, or non-additive. Store the additive parts of a ratio, and compute the ratio from totals.
  • There are four kinds of fact table: transaction, periodic snapshot, accumulating snapshot, and factless. Only the accumulating snapshot updates its rows.
  • Dimensions are wide, flat, and written in business words. They flatten hierarchies into plain columns and use integer surrogate keys.
  • Special Unknown and Not applicable rows keep every fact joinable and every total complete.
  • A star loads dimensions first and facts second. Inferred members link facts that arrive before their dimension rows.
  • Never join two fact tables directly. Drill across: total each fact separately, then join the results on conformed dimensions.
  • Conformed dimensions, planned with a bus matrix, connect stars into a galaxy schema.
