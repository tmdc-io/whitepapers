Part I · Chapter 4

Time and history

This chapter covers
  • Business time and record time, the two clocks of history
  • Three ways to keep history: snapshots, change logs, and versioned rows
  • Effective dating with half-open intervals
  • Bitemporal data, with both clocks on every row
  • As-of queries for each clock
  • Insert-only storage compared with update in place

Operational systems live in the present. They know Maria's address now. An analytical model lives in the past as well. It must know where Maria lived when she placed each order, and sometimes also what the company believed about her address on a given day. Those two questions sound the same. They are not, and the difference decides several of the stress tests in this book.

This chapter explains the two clocks of history, the main ways to store history, and how to query it. Every pattern chapter refers back to these ideas when it shows how its pattern keeps change.

4.1Two clocks

Business time, also called valid time, is when something was true in the real world. Maria lived at 14 Elm St in Oakland until 10 March 2025. That date belongs to her life, not to any computer.

Record time, also called transaction time or system time, is when a database learned or recorded something. Lantern's CRM recorded her move on 14 March, four days later. If a clerk then fixed a typo in the move date on 21 March, the database has a third date: when its belief changed.

Most questions use business time. "Revenue by the customer's city at the time of the order" needs to know where Maria lived on each order date. Audits and investigations use record time. "What address did the company have on file when it sent the 18 March mailing?" needs to know what the database believed on 18 March, even if that belief was later corrected.

NoteDifferent patterns and books use different names for the two clocks: valid time and transaction time, effective date and load date, business time and system time. This book says business time and record time, and uses the column names valid_from and recorded_at or load_dts.

4.2Three ways to keep history

There are three basic ways to keep history. Every pattern in this book uses one or more of them.

Snapshots copy the whole state at regular times. Every night, Lantern copies the full customer table and stamps it with the date. History is complete at each snapshot time and missing in between. If Maria moved and moved back within one day, the snapshots do not show it. Snapshots are simple and expensive: 600,000 customer rows a night is about 220 million rows a year, almost all unchanged.

Change logs store each change as an event. "On 14 March, the CRM changed Maria's address from Elm St to Bay Rd." A log is small and complete, but it is hard to query, because the state on any date must be rebuilt by replaying the changes up to that date.

Versioned rows store one row per version of an entity, with the period during which that version was true. This is the most common method in analytical models. A star schema's slowly changing dimension, a Data Vault satellite, and an anchor model's historized attribute are all versioned rows.

4.3Effective dating

Table 4.1 uses effective dating: each row carries the start and end of its period. Two small decisions make effective dating reliable.

Use half-open intervals. A half-open interval includes its start and excludes its end, written [valid_from, valid_to). Maria's Oakland row is valid from 1 April 2022 up to, but not including, 10 March 2025. Her San Jose row starts on 10 March. Each end equals the next start, with no gaps and no overlaps. A query for any date finds exactly one row with valid_from <= d AND d < valid_to.

WarningAvoid closed intervals with an end date of "the day before", such as Oakland until 9 March. They break as soon as a value can change twice in one day, or when the column holds timestamps instead of dates. They also tempt people to write BETWEEN, which includes both ends and returns two rows on the change date.

Use a fixed date for "still true". The current row needs an end. Many teams use a far-future date such as 9999-12-31 instead of a null, so that the same range condition works on every row without a special case.

Listing 4.1 The address valid on each order date

SELECT o.order_id, o.order_date, a.address
FROM   sales_order o
JOIN   customer_address a
  ON   a.customer_id = o.customer_id
 AND   a.valid_from <= o.order_date                   -- started on or before1
 AND   o.order_date <  a.valid_to;                    -- ended after2
  1. 1The start is included. An order on 10 March gets the San Jose row.
  2. 2The end is excluded, with a strict less-than. An order on 10 March does not also match the Oakland row.

4.4Bitemporal data

Versioned rows with business dates answer "where did Maria live on 12 March?" They cannot answer "what did we believe on 18 March about where she lived on 12 March?" For that, each row needs a second period: when the database held that belief. A table with both periods is bitemporal. Richard Snodgrass's Developing Time-Oriented Database Applications in SQL is the classic reference.

Here is what happened at Lantern. Maria moved on 10 March. On 14 March, a clerk recorded the move, but typed 17 March as the move date. On 21 March, another clerk fixed the date to 10 March. Table 4.2 shows the bitemporal rows that result.

Most models in this book keep only one clock fully. A star schema with type 2 dimensions keeps business time. A Data Vault keeps record time on every row and business time in its data. Bitemporal designs keep both, at the cost of more columns, more rows, and more careful queries. Stress test 7 in chapter 5 asks each pattern for exactly this kind of answer.

4.5As-of queries

An as-of query asks for data as it was at a point in time. With two clocks, there are two kinds. Listing 4.2 shows both on Table 4.2.

Listing 4.2 As-of queries on each clock

-- Business time: where did Maria live on 12 March, by today's knowledge?
SELECT address
FROM   customer_address_bt
WHERE  customer_id   = 'C-000123'
AND    valid_from    <= DATE '2025-03-12' AND DATE '2025-03-12' < valid_to
AND    recorded_to   = DATE '9999-12-31';                  -- current belief only1

-- Both clocks: what did we believe on 18 March about 12 March?
SELECT address
FROM   customer_address_bt
WHERE  customer_id   = 'C-000123'
AND    valid_from    <= DATE '2025-03-12' AND DATE '2025-03-12' < valid_to
AND    recorded_from <= DATE '2025-03-18' AND DATE '2025-03-18' < recorded_to;2
  1. 1Today's view keeps only the rows whose record period is still open. The answer is San Jose.
  2. 2The same half-open rule, applied to the second clock. The answer is Oakland.
NoteThe SQL:2011 standard added temporal tables, with periods for both clocks and clauses for as-of queries. Support differs between engines, so this book writes the conditions out by hand, as in Listing 4.2. The result is the same.

4.6Insert-only storage and update in place

There are two broad ways to apply a change. Update in place overwrites the old value, or closes the old row by changing its end date. Insert-only storage never changes a row. It adds a new row for every change and leaves the old rows exactly as they were.

Update in place keeps tables smaller and queries simpler. Its cost is that some history is lost. If a load closes a row by updating valid_to, the table no longer shows when that update happened, so record time is gone unless another column keeps it.

Insert-only storage keeps every version that ever arrived, with the time that it arrived. It makes loads simple and safe to repeat, and it makes audits possible. Its costs are larger tables and queries that must find the newest row for each key. It also makes deletion hard, because removing a row breaks the rule that the store only grows. Stress test 12 in chapter 5 is about exactly that conflict.

Summary

  • History has two clocks: business time, when something was true, and record time, when the database learned it.
  • History can be kept as snapshots, change logs, or versioned rows. Versioned rows are the common choice in analytical models.
  • Use half-open intervals, [valid_from, valid_to), and a far-future end date for current rows.
  • A bitemporal table keeps both periods on every row. It can answer what was true, and what was believed, on any date.
  • As-of queries apply the same range condition to one clock or to both.
  • Insert-only storage keeps every version and its arrival time. It makes audits and reloads safe, and makes deletion hard.
