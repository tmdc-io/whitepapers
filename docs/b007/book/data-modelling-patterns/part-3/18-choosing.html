Choosing a pattern

This chapter covers
  • Starting from the tests that matter to you
  • Six questions that point to a design
  • Typical situations and their designs
  • Signs that a design no longer fits
  • Moving from one pattern to another

Every pattern in this book is the right answer somewhere. The star is the right answer for most analysts, Data Vault for many changing sources under audit, anchor modeling for a schema in constant motion, the activity schema for customer journeys. The mistake is not choosing a weaker pattern. It is choosing a pattern for a situation that is not yours, usually because another company used it, or because it was the pattern the team knew.

This chapter turns the comparison into a decision. It starts from the stress tests that matter to you, asks six questions, and maps the answers to a design.

18.1Start from the tests that matter

Before choosing, go through the twelve tests in chapter 5 and mark each one as critical, useful, or irrelevant for your company. Then ask how often each event happens. Lantern buys a company every few years, adds a source column every few months, and corrects payroll every week. A test that happens weekly matters more than one that happens once a decade, even if the rare one is dramatic.

The weighting exercise in section 16.5 does this with numbers. The questions below do it with judgment, which is usually enough.

18.3Typical situations

A small team with one or two stable sources. Use stars, built from a persistent staging area, and wide tables for dashboards. Add type 2 dimensions where history matters. This is the lowest-effort design that still keeps a full record, and it can grow into a vault later. See chapters 7 and 10.

A regulated company with many changing sources. Use a Data Vault core, a business vault for rules, and stars and wide tables as marts. Invest in automation from the first month. Design personal-data satellites before the first load. See chapters 11 and 12.

A company whose main questions are about customer behavior. Use an activity stream for customers, beside stars for finance and operations. Make identity stitching part of every load. See chapter 14.

A domain whose schema changes constantly. Use an anchor core with generated views, and stars on top for analysts. Test join elimination on your engine first. See chapter 13.

Self-service users who combine many business processes. Use a galaxy of stars with a unified bridge on top, so that any combination of fields gives correct totals. See chapter 15.

A company that wants one consistent record above all. Use a normalized core with dependent marts, owned by an enterprise modeling team. Add record time on purpose, or the audit test fails. See chapter 6.

Machine learning features. Use wide tables at the grain of the prediction, such as one row per customer, built with as-of logic from any core that keeps history. See section 10.6.

18.4Signs that a design no longer fits

Designs that fit at the start stop fitting as a company changes. These signs mean it is time to revisit the choice.

The same dimension is rebuilt again and again. If every new source, merger, or matching fix means rebuilding the customer dimension and reloading facts, the integration work has outgrown a star built straight from sources. A record layer underneath, persistent staging or a vault, makes those rebuilds safe and cheap.

Audits cannot be answered. If someone asks what the warehouse said on a past date and nobody can answer, record time is missing. Add load times to the core, bitemporal dimensions where audits focus, or a vault.

Totals disagree between reports. If finance and marketing get different numbers for the same measure, either the definitions differ, which is a rule problem for a business layer, or self-service queries fall into chasm traps, which is a problem for drill-across rules or a unified bridge.

Every small change becomes a project. If adding one source column takes weeks because it touches many tables and reports, the model is too rigid for the rate of change. Look at the vault or anchor patterns for the core.

The model is more complex than the questions. If a team spends most of its time running layers that few people query, the design is heavier than the situation. Remove layers that no test needs.

18.5Moving between patterns

Changing a pattern does not mean starting again. Most moves add a layer below or above what exists.

From stars to a vault core. Start a persistent staging area now, if there is none. Build hubs, links, and satellites from it, one business process at a time. Rebuild each star from the vault, and compare it with the old star until they match. Then switch the loads.

From a normalized core to a vault core. The normalized core's entities suggest the hubs, and its associative tables suggest the links. Its effective-dated tables become satellites. The key cross-reference becomes the source of the first same-as links.

From wide tables to stars. The wide table's grain becomes the fact grain. Each group of repeated attributes becomes a dimension. Build the stars, then rebuild the wide tables from them, so that dashboards do not change.

Adding an activity stream. Each activity is a transformation over existing tables, such as the sales fact or the vault's order links. The stream can be built beside any core without changing it.

NoteEvery one of these moves is easier when the source data was kept. A persistent staging area is the single decision that keeps the most future options open, whatever pattern you choose today.

Summary

  • Choose a pattern for your situation, not for its popularity. Start from the stress tests that matter, and how often they happen.
  • Six questions, about sources, audits, users, customer journeys, the team, and schema change, point to a design.
  • Typical designs are stars for small teams, a vault core for regulated multi-source companies, an activity stream for customer behavior, an anchor core for constant schema change, a unified bridge for self-service, and a normalized core for one consistent record.
  • Repeated rebuilds, unanswerable audits, disagreeing totals, slow small changes, and unused layers are signs to revisit the design.
  • Moves between patterns usually add a layer. A persistent staging area keeps the most options open.
