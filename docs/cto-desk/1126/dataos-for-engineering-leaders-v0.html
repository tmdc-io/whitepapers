DataOS for Engineering Leaders

17 November 2026

This document aligns us on one idea. DataOS can become the platform that the engineering leader of a non-tech company uses to bring AI to the whole organization and drive its adoption.

The engineering leader is the person who owns where the company's software runs and how it is built. This is the CIO, the CTO or the chief architect. At smaller companies, it is the VP of engineering. An agent is an AI program that does tasks for a person. The document gives the hypothesis, the competition, the lessons from other companies, the market and the brand that the idea needs.

What we are aligning on
  • The buyer: the engineering leader of a non-tech company, who must now drive AI adoption across the organization (section 1).
  • The gap: no company gives engineers one neutral place to build, run and govern agents on the company's own systems. DataOS already has the pieces underneath: identity, access, policy and data products (section 3).
  • The motion: DataOS is SaaS. Engineers start for free, and the engineering leader pays when an agent reaches private systems and goes live for colleagues. Engineers sell on product signals, not business sellers (section 5).
  • The identity: DataOS becomes its own brand, and one pod runs it end to end. Modern keeps the business user (section 5).

1Engineering leaders need an AI platform

Hypothesis

Modern sold DataOS mainly to business users, and the response was lukewarm. AI creates a new need. The engineering leaders of non-tech companies need a platform where the whole organization builds, runs and governs AI agents and apps. DataOS fits that need and can capture that market.

What our deals show

Our deal records show a pattern. In every account, a technical owner decides where DataOS runs, which data it can use and who controls it. When a deal went around that person, it stalled. Where that person pays for DataOS, the account is live, even when the work is hard.

The business sponsor wanted the result. The person who owns where software runs did not want another platform that someone else chose. That person now has a much larger problem than before, and DataOS can solve it.

What changed with AI

People in every function now build tools with AI. Of US workers who built a work tool with AI coding tools in the past year, 34% have no formal programming background (Zapier, May 2026). 84% of CIOs say that employees create agents and apps faster than IT can govern them. 81% of CIOs do not fully see the agents built outside approved systems (Dataiku, 2026).

This demand comes to the engineering leader, who is accountable for it. Two-thirds of 2,000 CIOs and CTOs say that they are accountable for AI systems that they do not fully control (IBM, Jun 2026). Of 307 technology leaders, 93% are worried about AI-built tools in production. Only 8% say that their governance is strong (Retool, Jun 2026).

So the engineering leader now has a new job: to help the whole company adopt AI safely. The measure of success is how much of the company uses AI safely. It is not how many requests the leader refuses. DataOS serves that job.

DataOS sits between the tools that colleagues build and the systems that the company already runs. The engineering leader owns this middle layer. Requests come in from the left. Governed access goes out to the right, across a line that only the engineering leader controls.

What Glean does, and what it shows us

Glean is the company nearest to the buyer that we want, so we look at it more closely. It started in 2019 as search across the documents, chat and tickets of a company, and it launched in 2021. Today it is a horizontal AI platform. Employees ask questions and build agents on company knowledge.

Glean sells to the CIO and the CTO. It respects the existing permissions of each employee. It connects to more than 275 business systems (Glean). It links people, content and work in a structure that it calls the Enterprise Graph.

Glean starts from documents and conversations, and it now builds agents on top of them. DataOS starts from the company's systems and governs agents built anywhere. Because this plan leads with agents, Glean is a closer neighbor than before. In 2026, Glean began to read cloud data warehouses such as Snowflake and to turn questions into SQL (Glean). It also offers a "Customer Hosted" version, which runs in the customer's own AWS or GCP account under Glean's management (Glean docs). Glean sends its context to other agents through MCP, which includes the agent tool of Snowflake (Glean).

4Free use first, then a sales team

Product-led growth means that people use a product before anyone sells it to them. Developer-tool companies that grew this way had the same shape. They grew slowly from free use for years. Then an outside event made the product urgent, and growth turned.

The companies that went deep into banks, retailers and manufacturers all added an enterprise sales team on top of free use. Most added it four to eight years after launch. Free use created the lead, and a seller closed it.

WorkOS: one checklist, sold to the developer

WorkOS started in 2019. The founder's previous company, Nylas, did not succeed with large companies, because it did not meet their security requirements (The Generalist). WorkOS turned that checklist into products that a developer adopts alone: single sign-on, user directories and audit logs.

WorkOS had about 200 customers in June 2022 and more than 1,000 paying customers in early 2025. Analysts estimate about $20M ARR in June 2025, six years after founding (Sacra). Then AI companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Cursor and Perplexity had to sell to large companies from their first day, and growth turned (Sacra interview).

One estimate puts WorkOS near $100M ARR in October 2026, from a single source. WorkOS raised $100M at a $2B valuation in March 2026 (WorkOS). At about $50M ARR, it still had only three sales reps (Knuckle Up podcast). The lesson for us is that WorkOS charges for what the customer's security review demands. Our equivalent is the engineering leader, who must pass the company's own security review before an agent goes live.

Vercel: open source as the funnel, AI as the turn

Next.js is free and open source. Developers start on the free tier, teams move to Pro at $20 per seat, and companies sign Enterprise contracts. For most of its life, Vercel sold to large companies through developers. In 2025, it hired a former Stripe executive as COO, when enterprise demand for AI apps grew.

Team and Enterprise accounts now give more than half of the revenue of v0, the AI app builder of Vercel (GIC). Customers include Nike, Walmart and PayPal. Vercel raised $300M at $9.3B in September 2025 (Business Wire).

Glean: sold top-down to the CIO, turned by generative AI

Glean took the opposite path to WorkOS and Vercel. It sold top-down to the CIO from the start, with no free tier. It first found its market with technology companies of 500 to 2,000 people, and moved to large companies later (Sacra).

Two lessons follow for us. First, a CIO buys a horizontal AI platform at a six-figure price. Second, it is easier to start with companies whose people already build. For this reason, our first funding checkpoint measures builder activity at firms like Gensler before we go wider.

Two warnings

Pivotal sold its platform top-down to banks, insurers and carmakers, with services attached. Developers never chose it. It lost when Kubernetes became the standard, and VMware bought it in 2019 for about 30% of its peak value (RedMonk).

Docker had the opposite problem. Developers liked it, but it did not turn that use into enterprise revenue. It sold its enterprise business in 2019 (SiliconANGLE).

What we take from this

Five lessons apply to DataOS:

  • Plan for a long, flat period. An outside event turns the curve. Our event is that every function in the company builds with AI at the same time.
  • At non-tech firms, free use creates the lead and a technical seller closes it. McKinsey found that most companies that adopt product-led growth get no gain. The winners add a sales team that acts on product use (McKinsey).
  • Charge for what the security review demands: identity, policy, audit and access to company data. Engineers can do without the rest, so the rest stays free.
  • Keep the paid layer on what a cloud provider cannot match alone: governed access to private systems, neutral across clouds and models.
  • Keep the free path for developers after sales arrives. Engineers who choose the product protected HashiCorp. Pivotal did not have them.

5How DataOS wins

This section describes how DataOS reaches the engineering leader and wins. It covers why the leader picks DataOS, how product-led growth plays out on SaaS, product-led sales and the entry products. It then covers where we land first and how we should think about price. It also covers the brand and the team that run the play. It uses the lessons from section 4.

Why the engineering leader picks DataOS

At the moment of choice, the engineering leader compares DataOS with an agent platform from a cloud provider, a data platform or Glean. Four reasons decide it for DataOS:

  • Agents reach private systems safely. A SaaS product that connects to systems behind the firewall, with governance, is the step that hosted tools cannot take (section 2).
  • DataOS governs agents built anywhere. Colleagues can build in Claude, Lovable, Glean or DataOS itself, and the leader sees and controls all of them in one place.
  • DataOS is neutral across clouds and models. A company with more than one cloud or data platform does not have to pick one vendor for all its agents.
  • Agents give correct answers. DataOS Data Products add business meaning when a company needs it. This is the path from working agents to trusted agents.

How product-led growth plays out

DataOS starts as SaaS, because self-serve growth needs a product that an engineer can open in minutes. A version that runs in the company's own cloud comes later, for companies that require it. It is not the default.

The free user is an engineer on the leader's team, or an engineer in a business unit. These are the people who receive the prototype of a colleague today. They start alone, with no sales call, and build or host one agent for one team. The free tier connects to data sources that are open to the internet, for example Snowflake, Salesforce or Google Drive, through a normal sign-in.

The wall, from section 1, is the point where an agent needs private systems behind the firewall and must go live for other colleagues. At the wall, the company installs a secure connector, turns on company sign-in, sets policy and gets an audit record. Only the engineering leader can approve that step, and DataOS gives the leader a governed way to say yes.

The entry products

Every company in section 4 had a concrete free thing that developers wanted first, for example Next.js or Terraform. DataOS needs the same, and it can have more than one. There are five candidates. They are hosting for AI apps and agents with private connections, governance for agents, orchestration and the agent runtime, agent identity, and security.

Some candidates are crowded. Orchestration has LangSmith, AWS, Google, Microsoft and Vercel. Okta and Microsoft give agent identity away inside products that companies already own. Large vendors are buying their way into agent security. Hosting with private connections and governance of agents built anywhere look stronger, but research decides.

Five tests choose the first entry products:

  • Engineers feel the pain on day one.
  • An engineer can adopt it alone in an hour.
  • It leads directly to the paid wall.
  • A cloud provider does not give it away.
  • It plays to a strength of DataOS.

If we align on this document, the pod researches the candidates and picks the first entry products. That choice is the next level of detail, and it comes after alignment.

Where we land first

We start with mid-size enterprises, roughly 1,000 to 10,000 employees, in manufacturing, distribution, retail, insurance and financial services. Their boards push for AI now. They run more than one data platform and have private systems that agents need. Their engineering teams can adopt a product alone, but they are too small to build their own platform. They also buy faster than the largest companies.

Our accounts at Lenovo, Otis and Weatherford show the same need at a larger scale. Gensler is a reference for the buyer, not a target segment. Professional-services firms come later, because partners decide slowly, and vertical products already take their AI budgets. The largest companies also come later, because they buy slowly and choose their cloud provider by default.

The vendor channel

A second channel has the trigger closest to the one that turned WorkOS. AI and software vendors win enterprise deals only if their agent can reach the customer's private systems safely. DataOS gives them that reach as a product that they build into their own agent.

The vendor's developer adopts DataOS, and the vendor's customer demands it, so the need comes from outside, as it did for WorkOS. Each deal that such a vendor closes puts DataOS inside another company. The pod runs this channel alongside the self-serve loop.

How we should think about price

The price follows the loop above. Six principles shape it:

  • Building is free, within usage limits. Engineers build and host agents on cloud data for one team without a contract.
  • Payment starts at the wall. A company pays when an agent reaches private systems and goes live for colleagues.
  • We charge for what the security review needs: company sign-in, policy, audit and governed access to private systems.
  • We test two models before we choose one: a price per agent or app in production, and a platform price with usage. A price per agent can make leaders count agents, and that can slow adoption.
  • An enterprise tier adds company-wide sign-on, support and, on request, a version in the company's own cloud.
  • The entry price is lower than a business-outcome deal and public on the site. We will test it with Gensler, Lenovo and current prospects.

An engineering leader who hears a business pitch sees a vendor that goes around them. That happened at Target and Aptos. A business user who hears a platform pitch sees something that they cannot buy.

So we split by persona. Modern speaks to business users. DataOS speaks to engineering leaders and their engineers. Each persona hears one story at every touchpoint, from the first ad to the first login.

Four rules keep the two motions apart:

  • Existing DataOS customers, such as Gensler and Lenovo, keep their contracts and their support from Modern. The pod offers them the SaaS product when it is ready, and they become the first public references.
  • The DataOS pod owns every new sign-up and every account that it creates.
  • Modern sellers do not pitch DataOS to engineering leaders. When a Modern deal needs a platform conversation, the DataOS pod runs it.
  • We agree before launch, in writing, how quota credit works when a Modern seller expands a self-serve account.

One pod runs the play

A pod is one small team that owns a goal from start to end. One DataOS pod runs this play, from the first sign-up to the paid account. It has its own goals, its own budget and its own decisions.

The pod has its own engineers for the SaaS product, onboarding and the entry products, because these are new product work. It depends on core DataOS engineering only for platform releases, and that dependency has a written release commitment. Each other handover slows the loop, so the pod takes nothing else from the rest of Modern.

Appendix BThe Gensler lessons

Gensler designs buildings. It does not sell software. Suri, its technology head, owns all platforms and IT and runs them to serve the business. Eric reports to him, and Eric's department pays for DataOS. Gensler bought DataOS to reduce friction in data work across the firm.

Before DataOS, every team that needed data waited for engineering to connect sources, set access and keep it running. After DataOS, Suri's team runs one platform for sign-in, access control and the lakehouse. Colleagues use data without a new project each time.

The work is not always smooth. In April 2025, Gensler reported serious issues after upgrades, and it asked to keep governance under its own control. Both facts show the same thing: the platform owner sets the terms.

The AI version of this job is the same job with a new kind of request. Suri's colleagues now want to build agents and apps. Each request raises the same questions: where it runs, which data it can see, who can use it and who audits it.

The same leader, the same job, a new kind of request

Why an engineering leader bought DataOS for data, and why the same leader needs it for AI

Then: friction in data work

  • Colleagues need data from many systems
  • Each request becomes an engineering project
  • The team approves access and compliance case by case
  • The leader buys one platform, so his team can say yes once

Now: friction in AI adoption

  • Colleagues build agents and apps with AI tools
  • Each app needs company data and a place to run
  • Security review delays 83% of AI projects, and 29% wait four months or more (EMA for Protegrity, Jul 2026)
  • The leader buys one platform, so his team can say yes once

Gensler proves that this buyer pays for a platform that removes friction for the rest of the company. It does not yet prove that the buyer pays for the AI version. It also does not prove that the buyer arrives through self-serve sign-up. Our first question to Suri is how many AI requests reached his team in the last 12 months, and what he did with them.
